That other pathway could be hydrogen propulsion. In this article we'll dive into the tech fundamentals, recent industry developments and frame it's potential role as part of our next-gen triad.
# How Hydrogen Works

Instead of storing electricity in batteries, hydrogen propulsion uses hydrogen gas or liquid as an energy carrier. Hdydrogen’s energy density is enormous. While today’s best aviation batteries approach 250 Wh/kg, hydrogen sits at roughly 33,000 Wh/kg.

But hydrogen introduces a new dimension: volume. It’s extremely light, but it takes up far more space per unit of energy than jet fuel—even in super-cold liquid form. Designing tanks to hold that volume without wrecking aerodynamics or payload is one of hydrogen aviation’s hardest problems.

Hydrogen aircraft development has forked into two main branches: fuel cells and combustion.

## Fuel Cells

These systems work by feeding hydrogen gas and air into opposite sides of a kind of “battery.” Hydrogen from onboard tanks is split into protons and electrons at the anode. The protons pass through a membrane, while the electrons flow through the aircraft’s electric circuit, generating power. At the cathode, the protons and electrons reunite and combine with oxygen from the outside air, forming water vapor and heat, which are expelled.

This actually resembles an all-electric platform more closely than most think. The rest of the stack (electric motors, compute, electronics, airframe) can remained unchanged.

The limits are also similar, but the inverse.

We're talked a lot about how current batteries don't have good energy density. They do, however have good power density. What little energy they do have can be exerted quickly to enable all-electric take-offs, for example. 

Fuel cells however, have energy density, but lower power density. You can store a lot of potential in less weight, but turning that potential into power quickly isn't possible today.
## Combustion

So far we’ve looked at a way to work with the electric stack to incorporate hydrogen. This next platform is working from current turbofan technology and incorporating hydrogen. Coming at it from the other way.

Take a traditional engine. Air is sucked in, compressed and ignored with jet fuel. Instead we ignite it with hydrogen gas. Both systems blow the resulting exhaust out the back for thrust.  

Hydrogen combustion has a higher power output and can potentially scale to larger aircraft more easily than fuel cells. However, it’s less efficient than fuel cells and, while it eliminates CO₂, it still emits NOₓ (a contributor to climate forcing) and water vapor at high altitudes, both of which have climate impacts. It also requires cryogenic storage (liquid hydrogen at -253 °C), adding complexity and bulk to aircraft design.

The leader in this space is Airbus, with their ZEROe program. They plan to strap a modified GE engine onto the side of an A380 and fly it next year.

# A Concurrent Revolution

It’s tempting to think of electric, hybrid, and hydrogen propulsion as stepping stones—one replacing the other over time. But that’s not what’s actually happening. What we’re witnessing is three technological revolutions unfolding at once, each attacking a different corner of aviation’s energy problem.

- These aren’t competing timelines—they’re concurrent fronts in the same transformation.
- Advances in one feed the others: battery-driven electric aircraft prove out electric propulsion architectures; hybrid programs mature power electronics and certification frameworks; hydrogen demonstrators tackle storage, distribution, and high-energy flight. Each technology fills in the map that the others can’t cover alone.
- Short, thin regional routes—sub-500 km sectors with small payloads—are where battery-electric shines. It’s quiet, efficient, and emissions-free, but range and weight are hard limits.
- Hybrid propulsion stitches over those gaps. It combines the maturity of turbines with the efficiency of electric drives, opening medium-length, low-density routes and easing the infrastructure transition.
- What makes this moment historic isn’t that one silver bullet has emerged. It’s that three fundamentally different propulsion paradigms are maturing simultaneously, each carving out its niche, each accelerating the others. The aviation system of the 2030s won’t flip from kerosene to one new solution overnight—it will layer these technologies to solve different parts of the network.

## **Fuel Cells**

  

Fuel cells generate electricity through a **controlled electrochemical reaction** between hydrogen and oxygen from the air.

  

Hydrogen enters on one side and is split into **protons and electrons**. The protons pass through a membrane, while the electrons flow through the aircraft’s circuit, producing power. On the other side, the electrons and protons reunite with oxygen, forming water vapor and heat, which are expelled overboard.

  

Structurally, this resembles an electric aircraft. The motors, control electronics, and much of the airframe can remain unchanged. The key difference lies in the **power source**.

  

Batteries and fuel cells have **opposite strengths and weaknesses**:

- **Batteries** have **high power density** (good for takeoff and climb) but **low energy density**, limiting range.
    
- **Fuel cells** have **very high energy density** but **lower power density**, meaning they can store a lot of energy in less weight but can’t deliver it all at once.
    

  

This makes fuel cells ideal for **steady cruise power**, often paired with small batteries that handle power spikes during takeoff and climb.

---

## **Hydrogen Combustion**

  

The other approach starts from today’s **turbofan engines** and swaps the fuel. Instead of burning kerosene, the engine burns **hydrogen gas**. Air is compressed, mixed with hydrogen, ignited, and expelled through the turbine to create thrust—much like a conventional jet.

  

Hydrogen combustion offers **high power output** and can scale to **larger aircraft** more readily than fuel cells. But it comes with trade-offs:

- It’s **less efficient** than fuel cells.
    
- It produces **no CO₂**, but does emit **NOₓ** and **high-altitude water vapor**, both of which affect climate.
    
- It requires **cryogenic storage** at –253 °C, adding weight and design complexity.
    

  

The leader here is **Airbus**, through its **ZEROe program**. It plans to mount a **modified GE Passport engine** on the rear fuselage of an A380 to flight test hydrogen combustion—a milestone originally targeted for next year.

---

## **Recent Challenges**

  

The past two years have brought **a sobering reality check**.

- **Universal Hydrogen**, once a poster child for modular hydrogen logistics, collapsed in 2024 and sold off its IP.
    
- **Airbus** delayed its hydrogen timelines, scrapped the A380 fuel-cell test plan, and pushed its **2035 commercial hydrogen target** well into the 2040s, citing slower-than-expected technology progress and the lack of a supporting ecosystem.
    
- **ZeroAvia** remains the most active fuel cell developer. Its ZA600 powertrain aims to power 10–20 seat regional aircraft by 2027, with retrofit agreements from carriers like United (which is eyeing the larger ZA2000 for regional jets). For now, **they’re carrying the torch** for hydrogen-electric retrofits.
    

  

These shifts reflect a broader industry sentiment: hydrogen remains promising, but **near-term timelines were too optimistic**.

---

## **Infrastructure Challenges**

  

Technology isn’t the only hurdle. Hydrogen’s success depends on **airport infrastructure**—an entirely new logistics layer.

- **Green hydrogen supply** remains limited and expensive.
    
- **Cryogenic storage and handling** at airports require new safety standards, ground equipment, and trained personnel.
    
- Certification pathways are still taking shape.
    

  

At the same time, OEMs and airlines are prioritizing **SAF (Sustainable Aviation Fuel)** and **ultra-efficient engines** like CFM’s open-fan concept as **the practical bridge to 2035**, pushing large-scale hydrogen adoption into the **post-2035 horizon**. Universal Hydrogen’s failure also exposed the **capital intensity** of hydrogen aviation without near-term revenue.

---

## **A Concurrent Revolution**

  

It’s tempting to view electric, hybrid, and hydrogen propulsion as **stepping stones**, each replacing the last. But that’s not what’s happening. We’re watching **three revolutions unfold simultaneously**, each tackling a different corner of aviation’s energy problem.

- **Electric** leads on short, thin regional routes—sub-500 km sectors with small payloads—where low cost and zero emissions outweigh range limitations.
    
- **Hybrid-electric** bridges the gap, combining turbine maturity with electric efficiency for medium-length, low-density routes.
    
- **Hydrogen**, through both **fuel cells** and **combustion**, targets longer ranges and larger aircraft, scaling zero-emission flight beyond what batteries can handle.
    

  

These aren’t competing timelines—they’re **concurrent fronts** in the same transformation. Advances in one feed the others:

- Battery aircraft prove electric architectures.
    
- Hybrid programs mature power electronics and certification pathways.
    
- Hydrogen demonstrators tackle storage and high-energy flight.
    

  

The aviation system of the 2030s won’t flip from kerosene to a single new technology. It will **layer** these three systems to serve different parts of the network.

  

This is what makes this moment historic: not one silver bullet, but **three propulsion paradigms maturing in parallel**, each carving its niche and accelerating the others.
